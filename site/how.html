How Litmus works

Litmus asks which known texts your text resembles. A small model reads the text and compares it with a bank of texts whose origin is known, and calibration then checks how unusual that comparison would be for each origin. Whatever it cannot rule out, it tells you.

Step 1

What the model reads

Litmus reads up to 8,192 tokens at a time, where a token is a word or a piece of one, and longer texts in parts. It keeps the text as written, with its formatting and code. This release is an English detector: almost all of the text it learned from was English, it was tested mainly on English text, and other languages are not supported yet.

It learned three origins from public datasets only: human writing, text written by AI models, and mixed text that a person and a model both wrote or edited. Every human text it learned from is dated before 2022, and no text was generated for it.

More detail: the model and its data

The model is a BitNet b1.58 encoder: every weight is −1, 0, or +1, and the values passing between layers are 8-bit integers, so its heaviest arithmetic is adding and subtracting.

Each layer looks at nearby tokens, in blocks that shift by half a block on alternate layers so that information still crosses them, which keeps the cost in proportion to the text's length. The model was trained from scratch on labeled pairs of texts: for every pair in a batch it learned to say whether the two share an origin, and each training text was shown in a new form every time (formatting, dashes, quotes, wrapping, a few synonyms, cut mid-sentence) so that how a text looks says nothing of who wrote it. Its tokenizer is ModernBERT's, which works on bytes, so no text is unreadable to it.

The texts were split into five sets by whole clusters of similar texts, so that near-copies never cross from training into the sets used to choose, test, or calibrate the model: training, validation, test, the reference bank, and calibration. Training includes the official training splits of some public benchmark families, and the test split of every public benchmark in the results is held out of all five sets.

How Litmus was trained, step by step

Step 2

The reference bank

Litmus does not ask a model "is this AI?" outright. It compares your text with a bank of reference texts of known origin and asks, for each one, how likely the two are to share an origin. Averaged over each origin's references, those likelihoods become the reading's three shares.

The bank holds only the model's vectors of those texts and a description of each, such as its source, the model that wrote it, its domain, format, and language. It never holds the texts themselves.

More detail: the pair head

The pair head turns a text's vector into a unit query q and key k. The same-origin score of texts a and b is scale·(qa·kb+qb·ka)/2+bias, the same whichever text comes first. It was trained on every pair across two batches of texts with the true answer for each pair, so it learned what makes two human texts alike, two AI texts alike, and the two different, across many sources and formats.

Per origin, the reading keeps the mean and the spread of the same-origin probabilities over that origin's bank. The normalized means are the three shares, and one minus the human share is the score. Every reading can list its five nearest bank texts with their descriptions, so that a person can see what it was compared with.

Step 3

How sure, and what Uncertain means

A raw score is hard to read, so Litmus compares every reading with calibration texts: texts of known origin and about the same length that it never trained on. For each origin it counts how many of that origin's calibration texts look less like that origin than yours does. If very few do, the origin is ruled out.

The origins it cannot rule out at the 10% level make the verdict. One origin gives Human, AI, or Mixed. AI and mixed together, with human ruled out, give AI or mixed: a model wrote or edited the text as far as the reading can tell, and it cannot say whether all of it or part. Human beside another origin gives Uncertain: the evidence cannot rule human out, and the words beside the verdict name what remains. None gives Unlike reference, because the text resembles none of the reference texts closely enough for a call.

The guarantee is concrete. Among human texts like the calibration texts, at most 5% are called not human at the 5% level. That bounds false flags of human writing, and says nothing about how much AI text is caught, which the results measure.

More detail: p-values, the chance of AI, and its range

The count is a split conformal p-value, p = (1 + k) / (n + 1), where k of the n calibration texts of that origin and length group read at most this much like the origin. Sets are reported at the 5% and 10% levels.

The chance of AI starts from the probability that the text is not human, which comes from inductive Venn-Abers calibration. It calibrates the score twice, once as if the text were human and once as if it were not, and the two answers are the ends of its interval, narrow where many calibration texts scored alike and wide where they are scarce. The calibration texts are not an even mix of human and other texts, so the probability and its interval are restated at even odds, for a text as likely human as not beforehand. The result is the chance of AI.

That interval says how well the calibration texts pin the number down, and for one text it is usually under a point wide. The larger uncertainty is that one score means a different chance in one source of text than in another. So the calibration also measures, for each source of its texts, how far that source's texts sit from the others' in each region of the chance, and takes the spread of those shifts across 37 human sources and 75 AI and mixed sources, apart from what each source's own sample size explains. The interval is then widened on each side by the shift that holds 90% of the pairings of one human source with one AI or mixed source, which is 4.2 times the odds. The widened interval is the chance's range, and the strip draws the chance as its mark and the range as its band.

A text that sits farther from its nearest bank texts than 99% of calibration texts of its length is flagged as unlike the reference, whatever its verdict. The calibration tables are a few kilobytes and ship inside the model's files.

Step 4

Long texts, and where the reading changes

A text longer than 8,192 tokens is cut into equal chunks, each read like a text of its own, and the verdict combines them. The tint over the text, a heat map in cells of 128 tokens, shows where the reading changes. The map shows where to look; the verdict comes from the chunks, never from the most extreme passage.

More detail: combining chunks and drawing the map

A text of n tokens is read as ⌈n / 8,192⌉ equal chunks. Each origin's p-value for the whole text is twice the chunks' mean p-value, capped at 1, which stays valid however much the chunks resemble each other (Vovk and Wang, Biometrika, 2020). The probability behind the chance of AI is the chunks' mean, and its interval is widened by how much they disagree before the range is made from it. When one chunk reads clearly as AI and another clearly as human, a separate mixed-document signal says which chunks.

The map's windows are 512 tokens wide and start every 128 tokens. The model keeps running totals as it reads, so each window costs a subtraction rather than a second pass. A cell is colored by the windows over it, between human, mixed, and AI, and underlined when those windows are unlike the reference. A window is read with its neighbors in view, so its reading is close to, but not the same as, the reading of those words alone.

What it cannot tell you

  • Who wrote the text, or why. It reads the words on the page, not the process that made them, so it cannot say whether a model was used with permission, disclosed, or used only to fix spelling.
  • How much of a mixed text a model wrote. The heat map shows where a long text's reading changes, and the verdict does not measure a share.
  • Anything certain about a short text. Short texts are less reliable: a sentence or two carries little evidence, so the chance of AI stays nearer the middle and the verdict is more often Uncertain.
  • Anything reliable about a language other than English. This release is an English detector: almost all of the text it learned from was English, it was tested mainly on English text, and other languages are not supported yet.
  • Anything reliable about text unlike its references. New models and unusual formats can fall outside what it has seen, and the Unlike reference verdict catches some of these, not all.
  • Proof. Some human text is called not human at the rate the level allows, and more often when it is unlike the calibration texts.

Where it runs

One engine, written in Rust, runs the same model in the desktop program for Windows, macOS, and Linux, the command line, and the browser page, where it is compiled to WebAssembly. Its fast paths for x86, Arm, and WebAssembly processors are tested to give exactly the same numbers as a plain reference version. On one processor core the program reads about 4,896 tokens a second and the browser page about 894, with no graphics card. The program reads a text on every processor at once, and the reading is the same to the last digit: about 33,269 tokens a second on the 16 logical processors of the same machine.
